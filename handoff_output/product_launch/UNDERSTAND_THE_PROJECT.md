# Understand and defend the project

**Read this first: about 12–15 minutes.** Then rehearse the slides aloud. This is the short guide; the report and viva file are references if a question needs more detail. Numbers below are saved simulation results, not observations of actual students.

## 1. Scope and objectives — what problem are we solving?

**What the professor assesses:** a clear system, decision, boundary and assumptions.

**Our answer:** “A hypothetical seller launches a reusable bottle to 300 connected customers. We compare three prices, two free-sample quantities and three recipient rules. We ask which tested plan earns more contribution profit, and how conversations and contact changes affect it.”

The original pitch studied social polarization: people influence opinions and sometimes change contacts after disagreement. Following the professor’s feedback **as we understood it**, we chose an application, used synthetic inputs and compared what-if scenarios. We kept that opinion/contact mechanism and added awareness, budgets, buying and costs. The original pitch is in `sources/`; its vague journal/year leads are not verified citations.

There is one product, one campaign and at most one paid purchase per customer. No stock shortage, delivery queue, competition, returns, repeat purchase or fixed overhead. Sixty periods are abstract simulation periods, **not sixty days**. The seller can choose the offer; the contact-replacement policy is an environment scenario they may not control.

**Be ready:** Why simulate? Because changing one launch decision can change news, beliefs, purchases and later conversations. A simulation follows that feedback and compares policies on the same generated market. It does not prove real demand.

## 3. Model building — what actually happens?

**What the professor assesses:** method, individual rules, components, important design choices and contribution.

### Components you can point to in AnyLogic

| Component | Role |
|---|---|
|Main agent|Creates the market, organizes interactions, updates the display and saves CSVs|
|300 Consumer agents|Each references its own budget, value scale, belief, awareness, ownership and experience|
|Cyclic Event `launchPeriod`|Calls `advanceLaunchPeriod()` every 0.05 animation-time units|
|Java methods in `ProductKernel`|Select contacts, share news, update beliefs, replace ties, buy and account for costs|
|Basic presentation shapes|Circles, lines, text and plotted values; they do not decide purchases|

This is custom agent-based logic, **not a queueing model built from process-library blocks**. A central scheduler is compatible with agent-based modeling: customers retain individual state and decisions. We sum individual purchases; we do not impose an aggregate sales curve. People do not physically walk; a meeting means a selected discussion between contacts.

### One campaign, in order

1. Generate customers and their network. Give 3 or 12 bottles to selected recipients. They become aware and set belief to their own experience. Free recipients incur costs but never count as paid buyers.
2. Select one random customer and one random current contact. An isolate skips the attempt. If neither knows the launch, nothing happens. If exactly one knows, news passes with probability 0.25. Any informed person can share, even a nonbuyer: **our assumption**.
3. If both know and the belief gap is below 0.20, both move 30% toward the other’s **old** belief. Example:0.40 and 0.50 become 0.43 and 0.47. This is bounded confidence: people listen only within a tolerance.
4. If the gap is at least 0.20, there is a 5% chance to replace that contact. A new person is either random (`h=0`) or within tolerance (`h=1`, random fallback if none exists). Similar-contact selection assumes access to modeled beliefs; we do not estimate them from real ratings. No self/duplicate ties. One deleted tie is replaced by one; the edge count stays 1,200, although an individual can become isolated.
5. After 300 attempts, one period ends. Every aware nonowner with **budget ≥ price AND value scale × current belief ≥ price** buys once, simultaneously. New buyers then set belief to personal experience. Repeat for 60 periods.

**Worked purchase:** value scaleRs 800 and belief 0.60 imply perceived worthRs 480. With budgetRs 450, this person buys atRs 400, rejectsRs 500. Ability to pay and willingness to pay are separate. Ownership and awareness remain; beliefs and contacts can change.

### Parameters in ordinary words

`s=0.25`: how often news passes. `d=0.20`: how different beliefs may be before influence stops. `μ=0.30`: how far beliefs move in an accepted discussion. `φ=0.05`: how often disagreement changes a contact. `h`: how a replacement is chosen. `q=0.8`: the underlying experience draw’s mean **before clipping**, not a real quality estimate. Unit costRs 250 applies to both free and sold bottles.

Thirty visual ticks ×10 attempts make one period. Animation speed is not customer behavior. `rep=1000` selects a reproducible market, **not 1,000 replications**. Default `policyIndex=6`:Rs 400,12 samples, many-contact recipients, random replacement. Index 24 changes that to 3 samples. Other behavioral defaults are in `ProductKernel.Config`, not separately editable GUI sliders.

**Contribution:** a transparent application with money, individual eligibility, separate reach/sales metrics, paired experiments and a diagnostic of a problematic assumption. Bounded confidence, adaptive contacts and promotional seeding are published prior work. Do not claim to invent them.

## 4. Basic output analysis — how do we measure success?

**What the professor assesses:** precise metrics, correct accounting and interpretation.

| Metric | Definition / pitfall |
|---|---|
|Awareness|Number who heard about the launch; not a purchase|
|Paid buyers|People who bought; free recipients excluded|
|Total owners|Paid buyers + free sample owners|
|Revenue|Price × paid buyers|
|Contribution profit|(Price − unit cost) × paid buyers − unit cost × free samples|

Default illustration:148 paid,12 free, priceRs 400, costRs 250. Revenue=Rs 59,200; cost=250×160=Rs 40,000; profit=Rs 19,200. One default run is **not** an average or the best policy.

Holding 12 samples, many-contact targeting and random replacement fixed, the 100-market means are:

| Price | Paid buyers | Profit |
|---|---:|---:|
|Rs 400|147.33|Rs 19,099.50|
|Rs 500|84.85|Rs 18,212.50|
|Rs 600|32.21|Rs 8,273.50|

A mean such as 147.33 averages whole-person counts across markets; no run contains part of a person. Almost all 300 become aware. More awareness would not explain the profit difference; fewer people satisfy the purchase conditions at higher prices. Evidence: `all_policy_summary.csv`.

## 5. Advanced output analysis — are the comparisons fair?

**What the professor assesses:** alternatives, what-if experiments, uncertainty and decision insight.

Initial grid:3 prices ×3 recipient rules ×2 contact rules =18 policies with 12 samples. A later exploratory extension adds 3-sample choices, giving 36. A separate 30-market pilot precedes 100 main markets:3,600 main/follow-up runs on **100 markets**, not 3,600 independent populations.

Each market is reused across policies. Separate fixed random streams generate graph, traits, recipients and events. Six random event draws are consumed on every attempt so primitive input randomness stays aligned; evolving contacts can still differ. This is **common random numbers**, used for fair paired comparisons. It does not guarantee identical encounters or automatic variance reduction.

After selecting provisional choices, we fixed four comparisons and ran 100 **fresh** markets (400 evaluations). For 95% intervals, resample whole markets 10,000 times. Do not resample customers as independent markets. Intervals describe simulation variability **under the assumptions**, not whether those assumptions match people. They are pointwise; many comparisons need caution.

**Results you must know:**

- Fresh markets:Rs 400/3 samples/many-contact recipients earns meanRs 21,711. Three versus twelve samples gains **Rs 2,854.50**,95% intervalRs 2,698.50–Rs 3,006. Stronger support for fewer samples among these choices.
- Many-contact versus random recipients gains only **Rs 31.50**, interval−Rs 112.50–Rs 177. The benefit is uncertain; this does not prove exact equality. Do not pay a large targeting fee on this evidence.
- Main markets: reducing samples savesRs 2,250 directly, plusRs 595.50 extra paid margin. Total ownership falls even while profit rises. The changed sales are a whole-network effect, not proof that every removed recipient would buy.
- Higher unit costs shift the **best observed tested** price:Rs 400 at costsRs 150/250,Rs 500 at 350,Rs 600 at 450. Since cost does not enter buying, we can re-account unchanged sales. This would be invalid if cost changes also changed customer behavior.
- With more separated groups, across-group seeding improves early profit byRs 393 at period 7 but has no clear late advantage at 60. Objective and horizon matter.

**Unexpected diagnostic:** better experience (`q`:0.6→0.95) reduced mean sales 168.74→130.58 with narrow tolerance on a fixed graph. At tolerance 1, sales instead rose 174.76→280.96. A belief 0.50 listens to 0.60 but rejects 0.95 at tolerance 0.20. We tested tolerance and rewiring rather than hiding this. It warns that the hard influence cutoff needs validation; it does **not** recommend worse products. Evidence: confirmation, cost, stress and quality CSVs in `sim/analysis/`.

## 6. Animation — what should the audience see?

**What the professor assesses:** a running visualization that explains the model.

Grey=unaware; blue=aware nonowner; orange=free sample; green=paid buyer. Flat colors encode ownership/awareness, **not beliefs**. A black line marks a selected actual meeting; before/after numbers show belief change. A blue highlight marks a new contact. Grey solid ties connect close beliefs; dotted ties connect differing beliefs. Not every eligible tie actually interacts. The lower plots show awareness/sales history and the current belief distribution. Group positions have no physical-distance or opinion-axis meaning.

Run default 6/1000, explain one meeting, then speed up to period 60 and check 148 paid/Rs 19,200. Say this illustrates behavior; tables average many markets. If the latest display fails, open the approved grid `.alp`. Keep the PDF and model offline.

## 7. Verification, limitations and recommendation — what can we defend?

**Verification:** did we implement our intended rules? We checked eligibility, no double buying, sample costs, no-information/price limits, graph invariants and readable/native agreement:16 product checks. Standalone Java produced 1,080 runs; default actual AnyLogic CSVs match its same-generator realization. Those are stronger than merely viewing an animation.

**Validation:** are the rules suitable for the real system? Literature supports the starting mechanisms, not the exact buying rule. The original HK opinion benchmark reproduces a finite-population pattern; exact adaptive critical thresholds remain partial. None validates bottle demand. Full scenario results come from the reference implementation; actual AnyLogic evidence covers one default market shown in two variants, **not the full engine grid**. The simplified display now also has a student-supplied completed screenshot. A still image does not prove every animation frame or an additional independent market.

**Recommendation to say aloud:** “Within our synthetic choices, start by testingRs 400 and 3 samples. Use simple random recipients unless finding highly connected people is cheap. Revisit price when costs change, and use group coverage when early reach matters. Before real use, validate the customer rules and include operating costs.”

Only 3 and 12 samples and three prices were tested: no global optimum. No empirical calibration, operating system, sales forecast or universal targeting claim. The degree range here describes peers, not celebrity influencers. Research, implementation, analysis and drafting were AI-assisted; understand and present the evidence honestly.
